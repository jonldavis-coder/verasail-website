AI agent roles

An AI agent role is a job with a goal, inputs, an output, tools and a handoff to a person. Clear roles are what turn a set of prompts into a workforce.

By Verasail LLC · Published November 16, 2026 · 9 min read

An AI agent role is a job description for software. It names the goal, the inputs, the output, the tools and the moment the work passes to a person. Without that contract, a pile of prompts looks like a workforce and behaves like a chat window. With it, you can staff a small digital team the same way you would staff a human one: one job at a time, with a clear handoff.

A role is a job

Naming a model is not naming a role. "Use the language model" tells nobody what will be produced, for whom, from which sources, or when the work should stop. A new teammate would ask those questions on the first day. An agent needs the same answers, written down, because it will not infer your approval rules from the company culture.

People already work this way. A portfolio manager, a product owner and a data steward are roles because the work is distinct, even when one person sometimes covers two of them. An agent filling one of those jobs should be just as distinct. The catalog on the AI workforce page is a menu of jobs, not a menu of models. You can buy a ready-made role or have one tailored. Either way, the job description comes first.

What to write down for every role

Keep the description on one page. If it needs a second page, the role is probably two jobs wearing one name.

  • Name. Use the business words people already say. "Reporting specialist" is clearer than a coined product name.
  • Goal. One sentence. "Keep the weekly portfolio status accurate and on time."
  • Inputs. The systems, documents and fields it may read. Name them.
  • Output. The artifact or the system change it produces, and what done means.
  • Tools. The actions it may take: read, draft, comment, open a ticket, update a field. Leave off anything it should not do.
  • Handoff. The condition that sends the work to a named person or to another role.
  • Limits. What it must not do even if asked. Sending an external message, changing a permission and approving its own work are common entries.
  • Owner. A person accountable for the instructions and for the quality of the output.

That list is the contract. Prompts and tool settings implement it. They do not replace it. When the business rule changes, the owner changes the contract first and the prompt second.

Start from the work

List the recurring tasks in one process. Mark the ones that repeat, depend on data you already store, and have a mistake that is easy to see. Group the marked tasks by the decision they support. The group is a candidate role.

A weekly status process often splits into three jobs. One gathers the metrics. One checks them against the agreed definitions. One drafts the narrative for a person to accept or edit. Those might be a reporting specialist, a data-quality check and a writer. You can fold the first two together later if the volume is small. Splitting them on paper first shows the handoffs. The articles on portfolio management agents and product management agents walk through two places this split earns its keep.

A filled-in example

Here is a reporting-specialist contract small enough to be real. The goal is a Monday pack that uses the agreed definitions. The inputs are the initiative list, the status field, the owner, the last-updated date and the risk log. The output is a draft pack, not a sent pack. The tools are read access to those sources and permission to write the draft into the shared folder. The handoff is a person on the portfolio team, who sends the pack. The limits are: do not email customers, do not change the source status, and do not publish if a required initiative has no update newer than the agreed age. The owner is the portfolio lead.

If someone later asks the same agent to rewrite the roadmap, the contract says that is a different role. Dashboard and reporting automation is the ready-made version of the gathering half. Whether the pack is fit to send stays with the person named in the handoff.

Roles that show up often

You do not need all of these. They are useful shapes for a first digital team, not a target headcount.

  • Portfolio manager. Tracks initiatives, dependencies, risks and a status summary for leaders. See Portfolio Manager AI.
  • Product owner. Drafts and tidies user stories, acceptance criteria and stakeholder updates. A person still prioritizes. See the Product Owner agent.
  • Reporting specialist. Refreshes the numbers and the recurring pack.
  • Data quality specialist. Runs the rules, flags anomalies and routes issues to owners. See data governance and quality.
  • Service desk. Watches for early signs of trouble and handles routine resolution inside its permissions. See the proactive service desk.
  • Business analyst. Turns a request into a clear specification and a process map.
  • Orchestrator. Routes work between the roles above so a failed step does not disappear. See how orchestration works.

The full set of examples, including financial analysis, delivery and data roles, is on the AI workforce page. Add supporting roles after the core workflow is real.

How roles work as a team

A workforce is a set of roles with handoffs. The data-quality check feeds the reporting specialist. The draft goes to a person, who accepts it or sends a correction back. The correction should change the instructions or the data, so next week starts from the fix. A rewrite that lives only in the sent pack teaches the role nothing.

Write the handoff as a condition. "If a required field is missing, assign the issue to the data owner and do not publish." "If the variance crosses the threshold the portfolio lead set, stop and ask." Conditions like these are what an orchestration layer can enforce. A sentence that says "use good judgment" cannot be tested, logged or improved.

A generative draft has no job boundary. A role does. Agentic AI, generative AI and RPA is the longer version of that distinction.

Boundaries that keep a role useful

  • One primary goal. Extra chores are how a role becomes a junk drawer.
  • Least privilege. Read access is the default. Write access is a separate decision, system by system.
  • No self-approval. The role that drafts is not the role that signs off when the consequence is real.
  • A visible queue. Someone can see what the role is waiting on.
  • An off switch a named person can use without filing a project.

What an agentic AI workforce is covers these building blocks. Where the boundary should include a person is the human-in-the-loop checklist.

When to add the next role

Add a role when a real handoff is being done badly by the role you already have, or when a person is still doing a repetitive job you can describe. Do not add a role to complete a diagram. Twenty roles and one working workflow is a poster.

Review the roles you have. Retire any whose output nobody uses. Merge any pair that always runs together with no decision between them. Update the inputs when a source system changes. The owner should be able to explain, in a few minutes, what the role did this week and what it refused to do.

Whether a role should be bought or built is a separate decision, covered in build vs buy for AI agents. If you want to sketch the first few roles against your own process, start from custom agent development or the ready-made roles on the products page, and talk with us about which jobs are actually yours.
